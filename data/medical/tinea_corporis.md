# Tinea Corporis  

## Definition / Overview  
- Tinea corporis (ringworm) is a superficial dermatophyte skin infection caused by fungi of the genera *Trichophyton*, *Epidermophyton*, and *Microsporum*.  
- Presents as annular, scaly plaques with central clearing and an active, erythematous, raised border. Often pruritic, with potential for confluence in immunocompromised individuals.  

## Epidemiology  
- Highly prevalent worldwide.  
- Common in children, particularly prepubertal age group.  
- Risk factors: excessive heat, high humidity, fitted clothing, close contact with infected individuals/animals, poor hygiene.  
- Immunocompromised individuals are more susceptible and at higher risk for severe manifestations like Majocchi granuloma.  

## Etiology / Causes  
- Caused by dermatophytes:  
  - *Trichophyton rubrum* (80–90% of cases)  
  - *Trichophyton mentagrophytes*  
  - *Microsporum audouinii*  
  - *Trichophyton tonsurans* (associated with tinea capitis and tinea corporis gladiatorum in athletes)  
  - *Microsporum canis* (linked to contact with cats/dogs)  
- Transmission via direct skin contact, contaminated objects, or soil.  

## Pathophysiology  
- Dermatophytes infect keratinized tissue.  
- Susceptibility linked to defects in innate/adaptive immunity, including defensin β4 deficiency.  
- Risk factors: diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, Cushing syndrome, excessive sweating, advanced age.  
- Histopathology: KOH preparation reveals septate, branching hyphae.  

## Clinical Presentation  
- Pruritic annular lesions with sharp margins, erythematous scaly edges, and central clearing.  
- Lesions expand centrifugally, often on trunk, neck, arms, or legs.  
- Inflammation severity varies; vesicles may occur at active border.  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- Clinical diagnosis via history and physical examination.  
- Confirmatory tests:  
  - **KOH microscopy**: Septate, hyaline hyphae (false negatives in 15% of cases).  
  - **Fungal culture**: Sabouraud dextrose agar; growth within 5 days to 4 weeks.  
  - **Dermoscopy**: Identifies species-specific features (e.g., corkscrew hairs in tinea capitis).  
- Antifungal susceptibility testing for refractory cases.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Nummular eczema, erythema annulare centrifugum, tinea versicolor, cutaneous candidiasis, subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus, pityriasis rosea, contact dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, seborrheic dermatitis, psoriasis.  
- Severe mimics: secondary syphilis, mycosis fungoides, parapsoriasis.  

## Management / Treatment  
- **Topical therapy** (localized infection):  
  - Clotrimazole 1% (twice daily for 2–3 weeks)  
  - Ketoconazole 2% (once daily)  
  - Miconazole 2% (twice daily)  
  - Naftifine 1% (once daily or twice daily)  
  - Terbinafine 1% (once or twice daily)  
- **Systemic therapy** (extensive/refractory cases):  
  - Terbinafine 250 mg/day for 2 weeks  
  - Itraconazole 100–200 mg/day for 1–2 weeks (take with food)  
  - Fluconazole 50–200 mg/day or 150–200 mg weekly for up to 4 weeks  
  - Griseofulvin 500–1000 mg/day for 2–4 weeks  
- **Resistant cases**: Fosravuconazole for terbinafine-resistant *T. rubrum*.  
- Address predisposing factors (e.g., hygiene, moisture control).  

## Complications  
- **Majocchi granuloma**: Deep dermal invasion via follicles, presenting as erythematous nodules/papules; treated with oral terbinafine (250 mg/day for 2–4 weeks).  
- Secondary bacterial infections due to delayed treatment/misdiagnosis.  

## Prognosis  
- Generally excellent with appropriate therapy.  
- Localized cases resolve in 2–4 weeks with topical treatment.  
- Recurrence possible with persistent risk factors (e.g., immunosuppression, untreated tinea pedis).  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Wear loose, breathable clothing; maintain skin hygiene (keep clean and dry).  
- Avoid sharing personal items (towels, clothing).  
- Wash bedding/clothing in hot water.  
- Avoid contact with infected individuals/animals.  
- Compliance with antifungal therapy despite delayed symptom resolution.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- **Clinicians**: Diagnose and prescribe therapy.  
- **Pharmacists**: Verify antifungal dosing, check drug interactions (especially azoles), and ensure baseline liver function tests for systemic therapy.  
- **Nurses**: Monitor compliance, counsel on medication use, and report adverse effects.  
- Collaboration required for managing immunocompromised patients and antifungal resistance.
