# Acute Otitis Media

## Definition / Overview
- Acute otitis media (AOM) is an infection of the middle ear space, representing a spectrum of diseases including AOM, chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM), and otitis media with effusion (OME). It is the second most common pediatric diagnosis in the emergency department following upper respiratory infections.

## Epidemiology
- More common in males than females.
- Peak incidence occurs between 6–12 months of life, declining after age 5.
- Approximately 80% of children experience otitis media during their lifetime; 80–90% have otitis media with effusion before school age.
- Less common in adults but occurs in subpopulations with childhood history of recurrent OM, cleft palate, immunodeficiency, or immunocompromised status.

## Etiology / Causes
- **Infectious Factors**: 
  - Bacterial: *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, non-typeable *Haemophilus influenzae* (NTHi), *Moraxella catarrhalis*.
  - Viral: Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), coronaviruses, influenza viruses, adenoviruses, human metapneumovirus, picornaviruses.
- **Risk Factors**:
  - Decreased immunity (HIV, diabetes, immunodeficiencies).
  - Genetic predisposition; mucin gene abnormalities (e.g., MUC5B).
  - Anatomic abnormalities (palate, tensor veli palatini, Eustachian tube dysfunction).
  - Ciliary dysfunction, cochlear implants, vitamin A deficiency.
  - Allergies, lack of breastfeeding, passive smoke exposure, daycare attendance, lower socioeconomic status, family history of recurrent AOM.

## Pathophysiology
- Begins as inflammation following a viral upper respiratory tract infection, involving mucosa of the nose, nasopharynx, middle ear, and Eustachian tubes.
- Edema obstructs the Eustachian tube, causing negative pressure, mucosal secretion buildup, and bacterial/viral colonization.
- Progresses to suppuration/purulence, clinically presenting as bulging/erythematous tympanic membrane (TM) and purulent fluid.
- Differentiated from chronic serous otitis media (CSOM), which shows thick amber fluid and retracted TM.

## Clinical Presentation
- Otalgia is a key indicator; non-specific symptoms include ear pulling, irritability, headache, disturbed sleep, poor feeding, vomiting, diarrhea.
- Fever (low-grade) in ~66% of cases.
- Otoscopic findings: TM erythema/bulging, purulent fluid (suppurative OM); intact vs. perforated TM.

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Physical Exam**: Otoscopy (pneumatic preferred for higher sensitivity/specificity).
- **Diagnostic Tools**: Tympanometry, acoustic reflectometry.
- **Laboratory Studies**: Rarely needed; consider sepsis workup in infants <12 weeks with fever and no source.
- **Imaging**: Reserved for suspected intratemporal/intracranial complications (CT/MRI).
- **Tympanocentesis**: For refractory cases to identify pathogens.

## Differential Diagnosis
- Cholesteatoma, fever in infants/toddlers, hearing impairment, pediatric nasal polyps, nasopharyngeal cancer, otitis externa, human parainfluenza viruses, passive smoking, allergic rhinitis, bacterial meningitis, gastroesophageal reflux, *Haemophilus influenzae* infection, HIV, mastoiditis, pneumococcal infections, primary ciliary dyskinesia, respiratory syncytial virus, rhinovirus, teething.

## Management / Treatment
- **Pain Control**: NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) or acetaminophen.
- **Antibiotics**:
  - First-line: High-dose amoxicillin (10-day course) for non-penicillin-allergic patients.
  - Penicillin allergy: Azithromycin, clarithromycin, cefdinir, cefpodoxime, cefuroxime.
  - Refractory cases: High-dose amoxicillin-clavulanate.
  - TM perforation: Ototopical antibiotics (e.g., ofloxacin).
  - Watchful waiting (controversial in the U.S.) vs. immediate antibiotics per guidelines (e.g., Netherlands).
- **Surgical**: Myringotomy with tube placement for ≥4 episodes in 12 months.

## Complications
- **Intratemporal**: Conductive/sensorineural hearing loss, TM perforation, CSOM, cholesteatoma, mastoiditis, petrositis, labyrinthitis, facial paralysis, cholesterol granuloma.
- **Intracranial**: Meningitis, brain abscess, subdural empyema, lateral sinus thrombosis, otitic hydrocephalus.
- **Developmental**: Conductive hearing loss may impair language/speech development in children aged 6–24 months.

## Prognosis
- Generally excellent; rare mortality in developed countries with prompt treatment.
- Prognostic factors: Number of episodes, seasonality (winter), complications.
- Recurrent cases may lead to high hearing loss/speech therapy needs.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Vaccination: Pneumococcal and influenza vaccines.
- Avoid tobacco smoke, promote breastfeeding.
- Educate on antibiotic adherence, symptom monitoring, and follow-up.

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Team includes primary care providers, otolaryngologists (surgical referrals), audiologists (hearing assessment), speech therapists (developmental delays), nurses (medication education), and pharmacists (drug counseling).
- Early diagnosis and collaborative management reduce complications and improve outcomes.
