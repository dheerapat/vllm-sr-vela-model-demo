# Onychomycosis  

## Definition / Overview  
- Onychomycosis is a fungal infection of the nail unit. When caused by dermatophytes, it is termed *tinea unguium*. The term encompasses infections by dermatophytes, yeasts, and saprophytic molds. An abnormal nail not caused by fungal infection is classified as a dystrophic nail. Onychomycosis affects both fingernails and toenails, but toenail infections are more prevalent.  

## Epidemiology  
- Prevalence estimates range from 1% to 8%, with increasing incidence.  
- Key risk factors include aging, diabetes mellitus, tinea pedis, psoriasis, immunodeficiency, and familial history.  
- Genetic susceptibility follows an autosomal dominant pattern.  
- *Trichophyton rubrum* is the dominant causative organism in the United States due to international travel from Asia.  

## Etiology / Causes  
- **Dermatophytes**: *Trichophyton rubrum* (most frequent), *T. mentagrophytes*, *Epidermophyton floccosum* (account for 90% of toenail and 50% of fingernail infections).  
- **Yeasts**: *Candida albicans* (2% of cases, primarily fingernails).  
- **Nondermatophytic molds**: *Fusarium*, *Aspergillus*, *Acremonium*, *Scytalidium*, *Scopulariopsis brevicaulis* (≈8% of infections).  

## Pathophysiology  
- Infection often precedes with asymptomatic, dry hyperkeratotic tinea pedis.  
- Microtrauma and occlusive footwear create a warm, moist environment, compromising the hyponychial seal and allowing fungal penetration.  
- Dermatophytes colonize the nail bed via the distal hyponychium, progressing proximally with onycholysis and subungual hyperkeratosis.  
- **Histopathology**:  
  - **Acute phase**: Spongiosis, acanthosis, papillomatosis, edema, hyperkeratosis.  
  - **Chronic phase**: Compact hyperkeratosis, hypergranulosis, acanthosis, papillomatosis, sparse perivascular infiltrate.  
- Elevated interleukin-6 and interleukin-10 in nail bed and bloodstream; human beta defensin-2 present in mycotic nails.  

## Clinical Presentation  
- **Common subtypes**:  
  - **Distal lateral subungual onychomycosis (DSLO)**: Partial distal onycholysis with subungual hyperkeratosis (most common).  
  - **White superficial onychomycosis (WSO)**: White, chalky deposits on the nail surface (easily scraped away).  
  - **Proximal subungual onychomycosis (PSO)**: Involves the nail matrix, associated with immunodeficiency (e.g., AIDS).  
  - **Total dystrophic onychomycosis (TDO)**: Advanced stage with severe hyperkeratosis, nail destruction, and ridging (highest risk of ulceration and infection).  
- Often accompanied by mild, dry, hyperkeratotic tinea pedis (misidentified as xerosis).  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- **Mycological tests**:  
  - **KOH wet mount**: 60% sensitive; differentiates dermatophytes from saprophytes.  
  - **Nail biopsy with PAS/Grocott methenamine silver**: 95% sensitive.  
  - **Fungal culture**: Specific but 60% sensitive; requires mycologist interpretation.  
  - **PCR**: 20% higher species-specific detection than culture.  
- **Dermoscopy**: Identifies subungual short spikes, longitudinal striae, and "Aurora Borealis" patterns. Differentiates from transverse onycholysis (microtraumatic dystrophy).  
- **Imaging/clinical**: Baseline nail measurements and photography to track progress (toenail growth ≈1 mm/month).  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Yellow nail syndrome  
- Drug reaction  
- Hypothyroidism  
- Nail malignancy  
- Psoriatic nail  
- Contact dermatitis  

## Management / Treatment  
- **Systemic antifungals**:  
  - **Terbinafine**: 76% mycological cure rate; complete cure rate 38%.  
  - **Itraconazole pulse dosing**: 63% mycological cure rate.  
  - **Fluconazole**: 48% effective.  
- **Topical agents**:  
  - **Efinaconazole**: 55% mycological cure rate; 18% complete cure rate.  
  - **Tavaborole/Ciclopirox**: 36–9.1% complete cure rate.  
- **Adjunctive therapies**:  
  - Periodic debridement, chemical nail avulsion, or combination therapy (topical + systemic).  
  - Prophylactic antifungal powder for tinea pedis.  
- **Monitoring**:  
  - Baseline LFTs (ALT/AST) and hepatitis screening (if endemic exposure).  
  - Avoid systemic therapy in patients with alcohol use disorder, hepatitis, or polypharmacy (risk of drug interactions).  
  - Terbinafine contraindicated with phenothiazines/pimozide (QT prolongation risk).  

## Complications  
- Cellulitis  
- Sepsis  
- Osteomyelitis  
- Tissue damage  
- Nail loss  

## Prognosis  
- Depends on infection type/severity, immune status (e.g., diabetes, HIV), treatment adherence, and underlying conditions (e.g., peripheral vascular disease).  
- Recurrence rate: 5–50%.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Wear proper footwear in public areas (pools, showers).  
- Maintain hand/foot hygiene; avoid shared towels.  
- Use gloves in wet occupations.  
- Prompt evaluation for nail changes; comply with treatment.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- **Primary care/dermatologist**: Diagnosis and systemic therapy management.  
- **Pharmacist**: Monitor compliance, drug interactions, and adverse effects (e.g., hepatic toxicity).  
- **Nurses/podiatrists**: Debridement, follow-up, and patient education.  
- **Surgeon**: Referral for nail avulsion in refractory cases.  
- Collaborative care to reduce recurrence and complications.
