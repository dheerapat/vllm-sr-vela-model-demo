# Acute Kidney Injury  

## Definition / Overview  
- Acute kidney injury (AKI), previously termed acute renal failure, denotes a sudden and often reversible reduction in kidney function, measured by increased serum creatinine or decreased urine output.  
- AKI is categorized into prerenal, intrarenal (intrinsic), and postrenal etiologies, which may overlap or interrelate.  

## Epidemiology  
- AKI occurs in **1% of all hospital admissions**, with an incidence of **2–5% during hospitalization** and up to **67% in ICU admissions**.  
- Contributes to prolonged hospital stays and increased patient morbidity.  

## Etiology / Causes  
### Prerenal AKI  
- Hypovolemia: hemorrhage, severe burns, gastrointestinal losses (diarrhea, vomiting, high ostomy output).  
- Hypotension from decreased cardiac output: cardiogenic shock, pulmonary embolism, acute coronary syndrome.  
- Hypotension from systemic vasodilation: septic shock, anaphylaxis, anesthesia.  
- Renal vasoconstriction: NSAIDs, iodinated contrast, amphotericin B, calcineurin inhibitors, hepatorenal syndrome.  
- Glomerular efferent arteriolar vasodilation: ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers.  

### Intrinsic Renal AKI  
- **Acute tubular necrosis (ATN)**: prolonged prerenal injury, aminoglycosides, vancomycin, amphotericin B, iodinated contrast, rhabdomyolysis, intravascular hemolysis.  
- **Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)**: beta-lactam antibiotics, NSAIDs, proton pump inhibitors, 5-ASA, infections, autoimmune conditions (SLE, IgG-related disease).  
- **Glomerulonephritis**: anti-GBM disease, immune complex-mediated diseases (SLE, post-infectious glomerulonephritis, cryoglobulinemia, IgA nephropathy).  
- **Intratubular obstruction**: monoclonal gammopathy (e.g., multiple myeloma), tumor lysis syndrome, hemolytic anemia, ethylene glycol.  

### Postrenal AKI  
- Bladder outlet obstruction: prostatic hypertrophy (older men), pelvic masses (older women), nephrolithiasis (younger patients).  
- Ureteral obstruction: tumors, blood clots, neurogenic bladder.  
- Urethral obstruction: retroperitoneal fibrosis, pregnancy, fecal impaction, pelvic organ prolapse, phimosis.  

## Pathophysiology  
- **Prerenal**: Reduced renal blood flow due to systemic hypoperfusion or selective renal hypoperfusion.  
- **Intrinsic**: Tubular-glomerular damage from ischemia (ATN), toxins, inflammation (AIN, glomerulonephritis), or intratubular obstruction.  
- **Postrenal**: Urinary tract obstruction leads to increased upper tract pressure, decreased renal perfusion, and eventual chronic interstitial nephritis.  

## Clinical Presentation  
- Sudden decline in kidney function, often with normal BUN/creatinine initially; decreased urine output is a key early sign.  
- Common causes in hospitalized patients:  
  - ATN (45%)  
  - Prerenal disease (21%)  
  - AKI superimposed on CKD (13%)  
  - Urinary tract obstruction (10%)  
  - Glomerulonephritis/vasculitis (4%)  
  - AIN (2%)  
  - Atheroemboli (1%)  
- Urine output patterns:  
  - Oliguria: suggests AKI.  
  - Sudden anuria: acute urinary obstruction, glomerulonephritis, vascular blockage.  
  - Polyuria: postobstructive nephropathy or tubular defects (e.g., AIN).  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- **Criteria**:  
  - **KDIGO**: Increase in serum creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or ≥1.5× baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥6 hours.  
  - **RIFLE**: Risk (Cr ↑1.5×, GFR ↓25%, urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 h), Injury (Cr ↑2×, GFR ↓50%, urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 12 h), Failure (Cr ↑3×, GFR ↓75%, Cr ≥4.0, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 24 h).  
- **Diagnostic tests**:  
  - Comprehensive metabolic panel, urine electrolytes, osmolality, albumin-to-creatinine ratio.  
  - Serum and urine protein electrophoresis (older patients) to evaluate for monoclonal gammopathy.  
  - Renal ultrasound for obstruction; CT for nephrolithiasis/urolithiasis.  
  - Urine microscopy: muddy brown casts (ATN), white blood cell casts (AIN), sterile pyuria (AIN).  
  - Biomarkers (limited use): NGAL, KIM-1, cystatin C.  
  - **Fluid challenge**: Differentiates prerenal vs. ATN if no contraindications.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Renal calculi, chronic kidney disease, hypovolemia, gastrointestinal bleeding, decreased cardiac output, urinary tract infection, urinary obstruction.  
- Elevated creatinine unrelated to kidney disease (e.g., creatine supplements).  

## Management / Treatment  
- **Fluid challenge**: First-line for prerenal AKI if no contraindications.  
- **Avoid nephrotoxins**: Adjust medications, discontinue offending agents.  
- **Volume overload**: Use diuretics (e.g., IV furosemide) for symptomatic management; does not alter oliguric ATN progression.  
- **Hyperkalemia management**:  
  - Dietary restriction, insulin/dextrose, beta-agonists, potassium-binding resins, calcium gluconate for ECG changes, dialysis if refractory.  
- **Renal replacement therapy (RRT)**: Indicated for severe hyperkalemia, uremic pericarditis, pulmonary edema, or electrolyte acid-base abnormalities. Options include hemodialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy.  
- **Etiology-specific treatments**:  
  - Vasoactive medications/colloids for hepatorenal syndrome.  
  - Immunosuppressives for glomerulonephritis/vasculitis.  
  - Steroids for AIN.  
  - Surgical intervention for postrenal obstruction (e.g., prostatic hypertrophy, stenting for calculi).  

## Complications  
- **Metabolic derangements**: Hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, hyperphosphatemia, hyponatremia.  
- **Volume overload**: Pulmonary edema, peripheral edema.  
- **Cardiovascular**: Heart failure, arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, pericarditis.  
- **Gastrointestinal**: Nausea, vomiting, anorexia, GI bleeding.  
- **Neurologic**: Uremic encephalopathy (lethargy, somnolence, cognitive impairment).  

## Prognosis  
- **Prerenal AKI**: Complete recovery if treated early; may progress to ATN if insult persists.  
- **In-hospital mortality**: **40–50% overall**, **>50% in ICU patients**.  
- **Long-term**: **12–15% may require permanent dialysis**.  
- **Prognostic factors**: Older age, APACHE III score, persistent creatinine elevation, multiorgan involvement, sepsis.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Avoid nephrotoxic agents (NSAIDs, contrast), dehydration.  
- Monitor blood pressure and adhere to prescribed medications.  
- Dietary modifications: Sodium/fluid restriction, low-potassium diet if indicated, adequate protein intake during catabolic states.  
- Close follow-up with nephrology and primary care providers.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- Collaborative care involving nephrologists, pharmacists (medication review), dietitians (nutritional guidance), and primary care providers.  
- Early recognition and intervention by all healthcare team members to reduce morbidity.  
- Patient education on avoiding nephrotoxins and adhering to dietary restrictions.
