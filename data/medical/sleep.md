# Sleep Disorders

## Definition / Overview
- Sleep disorders are conditions that disturb normal sleep patterns, leading to inadequate or non-restorative sleep. They interfere with physical, mental, social, and emotional functioning and impact overall health, safety, and quality of life. The International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition (ICSD-3), categorizes sleep disorders into:  
  - Insomnia  
  - Sleep-disordered breathing  
  - Central disorders of hypersomnolence  
  - Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders  
  - Parasomnias  
  - Sleep-related movement disorders  

## Epidemiology
- **Insomnia**: Affects ~10% of adults, with higher prevalence in women (17.6% vs. 10.1% in men). Chronic in >40% with severe symptoms.  
- **Sleep-disordered breathing (SDB)**: Prevalence increases with age; ~50% of older adults report sleep problems. Men have higher prevalence than premenopausal women (2:1). Wisconsin Sleep Cohort Study showed increases from 26.4% to 33.9% in men and 13.2% to 17.4% in women over time.  
- **Central hypersomnolence**: Narcolepsy prevalence ~44.3/100,000; idiopathic hypersomnia ~10.3/100,000.  
- **Circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders**:Overall prevalence 0.13–0.17%, higher in adolescents (7–16% for delayed sleep phase syndrome).  
- **Parasomnias**: NREM parasomnias ~3%, nightmares ~43.8%, REM sleep behavior disorder ~8.7/100,000.  
- **Sleep-related movement disorders**: Restless leg syndrome (RLS) affects 9% women vs. 5.4% men. Periodic limb movement disorder ~40/100,000.  
- **Racial/ethnic differences**: Hispanics and Chinese have higher odds of SDB and short sleep; Black individuals have 1.8x higher risk of sleep apnea syndrome vs. White individuals.  

## Etiology / Causes
- **Insomnia**: Environmental, genetic, psychological, and behavioral factors leading to hyperarousal.  
- **Sleep-disordered breathing**: Obesity, upper airway mechanics, breathing control issues. Includes obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), central sleep apnea (CSA), obesity hypoventilation syndrome.  
- **Central hypersomnolence**: Narcolepsy type 1/2, idiopathic hypersomnia, Kleine-Levin syndrome, hypersomnia due to medical/psychiatric disorders, medication/substance use, sleep insufficiency.  
- **Circadian rhythm disorders**: Misalignment with environment (shift work, jet lag) or altered internal circadian timing (delayed/advanced sleep phase syndromes).  
- **Parasomnias**: Environmental, genetic, or gene-environment interactions. Subtypes: NREM-related (confusional arousals, sleepwalking) and REM-related (RBD, nightmares).  
- **Sleep-related movement disorders**: Restless leg syndrome (familial/genetic or iron deficiency).

## Clinical Presentation
- **Insomnia**: Difficulty falling/staying asleep (≥30 minutes), daytime consequences (fatigue, sleepiness, poor attention, irritability).  
- **Hypersomnia**: Excessive daytime sleepiness, unintentional sleep episodes, cognitive impairment. Narcolepsy may include cataplexy, hallucinations, sleep paralysis.  
- **Unusual behaviors**: RBD (kicking, punching during dreams), sleepwalking, sleep-related eating, night terrors, confusional arousals. Symptoms exacerbated by stress, sleep deprivation, or medications (e.g., z-drugs).  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Questionnaires**: Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), Fatigue Severity Scale (FSS), Insomnia Severity Index (ISI).  
- **Sleep diary**: Tracks bedtime, awakenings, naps.  
- **Sleep studies**: Polysomnography (PSG, gold standard for OSA), home sleep apnea testing (HSAT).  
- **Laboratory tests**: Arterial blood gases, thyroid function, iron studies, CSF hypocretin-1 (for narcolepsy).  
- **Actigraphy**: Assesses sleep-wake cycles for circadian disorders.  
- **Multiple sleep latency testing (MSLT)**: Measures daytime sleepiness severity.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- PTSD, depression, anxiety disorder, bipolar disorder, opioid abuse, alcoholism, stimulant abuse, COPD, hyperthyroidism.  

## Management / Treatment  
- **Insomnia**:  
  - **Non-pharmacological**: CBT-I (gold standard), sleep restriction, stimulus control, relaxation techniques, sleep hygiene education.  
  - **Pharmacological**: Histamine H1 blockers (avoid in elderly), benzodiazepines, z-drugs (caution: black box warning for complex behaviors), melatonin agonists (ramelteon), orexin antagonists (suvorexant).  
- **OSA**: PAP therapy, lifestyle changes, oral appliances, surgery.  
- **Narcolepsy**: Modafinil (first-line), stimulants (amphetamines), anti-depressants for cataplexy, sodium oxybate.  
- **Circadian disorders**: Light therapy, melatonin.  
- **RLS**: Gabapentin enacarbil.  

## Complications  
- Increased risk of accidents, mood/anxiety disorders, cognitive decline, cardiovascular diseases (e.g., hypertension, heart failure), cerebrovascular events (linked to periodic limb movement disorder).  

## Prognosis  
- Varies by cause. Insomnia related to OSA resolves with treatment; chronic insomnia correlates with depression/anxiety. Persistent hypersomnia despite therapy may indicate poorer outcomes.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Sleep hygiene: Regular sleep schedule, avoid screens/caffeine, optimal sleep environment.  
- Caution with sedative-hypnotics (avoid driving/operating machinery).  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- Team includes clinicians, specialists (pulmonology, neurology), pharmacists, nurses, psychologists, dieticians, surgical consults.  
- Coordination for sleep studies, medication management, behavioral therapy, and patient education is essential for optimal outcomes.
