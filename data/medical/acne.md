# Acne Vulgaris  

## Definition / Overview  
- Chronic inflammatory disorder of the pilosebaceous unit with a prolonged course, commonly presenting with comedones, papules, pustules, and nodules. Typically affects the face, upper arms, trunk, and back. Can occur at any age but most common in adolescents.  

## Epidemiology  
- Prevalence: 35%–90% in adolescents.  
- Onset: Begins as early as 7–12 years (preadolescent acne), usually resolves by the third decade.  
- Gender: More common in males during adolescence; postadolescent acne predominantly affects females.  
- Geographic: Higher prevalence in urban vs. rural populations.  
- Ethnicity: Asians and Africans more likely to develop severe acne; mild acne more common in White populations.  
- Neonates: Acne can occur but typically resolves spontaneously.  
- Infants/children: Rare; may indicate underlying conditions.  

## Etiology / Causes  
- Primary factors: Hypersensitivity of sebaceous glands to androgens, *Cutibacterium acnes* (C acnes), inflammation.  
- Contributory factors:  
  - Medications (lithium, steroids, anticonvulsants).  
  - Occlusive wear (e.g., backpacks, headbands).  
  - Oil-based cosmetics, facial massage.  
  - Endocrine disorders (polycystic ovarian syndrome, pregnancy).  
  - Genetic factors (50–90% heritability).  
  - Diet (high milk consumption, high glycemic load).  
  - Psychological stress.  
  - Insulin resistance.  
- Chocolate does not exacerbate acne.  

## Pathophysiology  
- **Four key events**:  
  1. Increased sebum production (seborrhea).  
  2. Follicular hyperkeratinization.  
  3. *C. acnes* colonization.  
  4. Inflammation.  
- **Role of androgens**: Stimulate sebaceous gland growth and sebum production. Most patients have normal androgen levels, but hyperandrogenism (e.g., PCOS) exacerbates acne.  
- **Role of *C. acnes***: Strains (e.g., phylotype IA) trigger innate/adaptive immune responses (TH17 cells, IL-17, cytokines). Biofilm formation linked to antibiotic resistance.  
- **Genetics**: Family history increases risk 3-fold. Upregulation of inflammatory genes (MMP-1, IL-8, beta-defensins).  

## Clinical Presentation  
- **Lesions**:  
  - Grade 1: Comedones (open/blackheads, closed/whiteheads).  
  - Grade 2: Inflammatory papules.  
  - Grade 3: Pustules.  
  - Grade 4: Nodules/cysts (nodulocystic acne).  
- **Distribution**: Face (T-zone in adolescents), neck, chest, upper back, arms. Adult women often on lower face/neck, linked to menstrual cycles.  
- **Variants**:  
  - Acne conglobata (severe nodules, interconnecting abscesses).  
  - Acne fulminans (ulcerating nodules, systemic symptoms).  
  - Acne excoriée (picked/scarring).  
  - Infantile acne (3–6 months, improves by age 1–2).  
- **Associated features**: Seborrhea, hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, irregular menses), postinflammatory hyperpigmentation (more common in darker skin).  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- **Clinical diagnosis**: Based on physical exam (lesion type, distribution).  
- **Laboratory tests**:  
  - For suspected hyperandrogenism (females): Testosterone, LH, FSH, DHEA.  
  - Acne fulminans: CBC, LFTs, ESR, CRP, cholesterol/triglycerides, pregnancy test, imaging for bone/joint involvement.  
- **Skin biopsy**: Not routinely required.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Rosacea (telangiectasia, no comedones).  
- Perioral dermatitis (perioral papules, lip sparing).  
- Demodex folliculitis (non-responsive to acne treatment).  
- Facial angiofibroma (tuberous sclerosis, pink/red papules).  
- Pseudofolliculitis barbae (beard area, curved hair).  
- Nevus comedonicus (grouped comedones, present at birth/childhood).  
- Sebaceous hyperplasia (umbilicated yellowish papules).  
- Adnexal tumors (flesh-colored facial papules).  
- Favre-Racouchot syndrome (photodamage, upper lateral cheeks).  

## Management / Treatment  
### **Mild Acne**  
- **Topical retinoids** (tretinoin, tazarotene, adapalene, trifarotene): Normalize keratinization, reduce comedones. Monotherapy for comedonal acne; combine with antimicrobials for papulopustular. Apply once daily (night), start every other night to reduce irritation.  
- **Benzoyl peroxide** (2.5%–10%): Comedolytic, antimicrobial. Avoid concurrent tretinoin (oxidation).  
- **Topical clindamycin/erythromycin**: Use with benzoyl peroxide to prevent resistance.  
- **Azelaic acid**: Comedolytic, antimicrobial, whitening (postinflammatory hyperpigmentation).  
- **Salicylic acid**: Alternative to retinoids.  

### **Moderate-to-Severe Acne**  
- **Oral isotretinoin** (0.5–1 mg/kg/day): Targets all pathogenic factors. Contraindicated in pregnancy (teratogenic). Monitor for myalgia, dry skin, hyperlipidemia, hepatic enzymes. Combine with steroids for flare prevention.  
- **Oral antibiotics** (tetracyclines preferred): Doxycycline, minocycline, sarecycline. Limit to 3–4 months; avoid in children <8, pregnancy. Adverse effects: photosensitivity, GI upset, pseudotumor cerebri.  
- **Hormonal therapies**:  
  - **Oral contraceptives**: FDA-approved options (ethinyl estradiol with norethindrone, norgestimate, drospirenone). Avoid in thrombophilic disorders.  
  - **Spironolactone** (25–100 mg/day): Anti-androgen; reduces sebum by 50%. Adverse effects: breast tenderness, menstrual irregularities. Combine with contraceptives.  

### **Management of Complications**  
- **Hyperpigmentation**: Photoprotection, retinoids, azelaic acid, hydroquinone, chemical peels.  
- **Scars**: Ice-pick (CROSS with TCA), rolling (subcision, fillers), boxcar (excision, lasers), hypertrophic/keloidal (laser, triamcinolone).  

## Complications  
- Psychological (depression, anxiety, low self-esteem).  
- Acne fulminans (ulcerating nodules, fever, arthralgia, osteolytic lesions).  
- Solid facial edema (Morbihan’s disease).  
- Gram-negative folliculitis (resistant to antibiotics, *Pseudomonas*, *Proteus*).  

## Prognosis  
- Generally good with treatment, but may leave scars.  
- Potential association between teenage acne and prostate cancer (limited evidence).  
- Scars are challenging to treat once established.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Gentle skincare (pH-balanced cleansers, non-comedogenic products).  
- Avoid harsh scrubbing/picking.  
- Limit high glycemic index foods/dairy (individualized).  
- Stress management.  
- Sun protection.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- Follow AAD and WHO guidelines for acne and hormonal therapy.  
- Pharmacists must adhere to iPLEDGE program for isotretinoin, educate on teratogenicity.  
- Nurses provide patient education, monitor treatment response.  
- Dermatology consultation recommended for severe/resistant cases.
