# Dysmenorrhea

## Definition / Overview
- Dysmenorrhea is defined as pain during the menstrual cycle, typically located in the lower abdomen and potentially radiating to the inner thighs and back.
- It is a common gynecologic problem that can negatively impact quality of life, with treatment options available to reduce associated morbidity.

## Epidemiology
- Prevalence ranges from 16% to 91% in individuals of reproductive age, with severe pain observed in 2% to 29%.
- Agarwal et al. reported an 80% prevalence in adolescents, with 40% experiencing severe symptoms.
- Associated with significant absenteeism (12% of monthly school/work activities) and economic impact (estimated 140 million lost working hours annually in the U.S.).

## Etiology / Causes
### Primary Dysmenorrhea
- Linked to prostaglandin (PG) hypersecretion.
- Risk factors: age <30 years, smoking, attempts to lose weight, abnormal BMI, depression/anxiety, longer menstrual cycles, younger age at menarche, nulliparity, history of sexual assault, uterine niche (post-C-section), heavy menstrual flow, family history of dysmenorrhea, disrupted social networks.

### Secondary Dysmenorrhea
- Associated with underlying pathology: endometriosis, large cesarean scar niche, fibroids, adenomyosis, endometrial polyps, interstitial cystitis, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), intrauterine contraceptive systems.

## Pathophysiology
- **Primary Dysmenorrhea**: Prostaglandins (PGF2α, PGE2) from endometrial tissue increase uterine contractions, causing tissue hypoxia and ischemia. Elevated COX-2 expression during menstruation drives PG production. Vasopressin and leukotrienes (C4, D4) may also contribute to contractions.
- **Secondary Dysmenorrhea**: Caused by structural abnormalities (e.g., endometriosis, adenomyosis) or infections (e.g., PID).

## Clinical Presentation
### Primary Dysmenorrhea
- Cyclic, crampy midline pelvic pain starting 2 years post-menarche or with ovulatory cycles.
- Pain begins within hours of menses, peaks 23–48 hours later, resolves within 72 hours.
- Associated symptoms: nausea, vomiting, headaches, dizziness, fatigue, sleep difficulties.

### Secondary Dysmenorrhea
- Pain may occur at any reproductive age, potentially with dyspareunia, menorrhagia, intermenstrual bleeding, or postcoital bleeding.
- Onset not strictly tied to menstrual cycle.

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **History/Physical**: Assess pain location, timing, characteristics, and associated symptoms. Pelvic exam indicated if secondary dysmenorrhea suspected or poor response to treatment.
- **Imaging**: Ultrasound as initial evaluation for secondary causes (endometriosis, fibroids, adenomyosis). MRI/Doppler if inconclusive.
- **Labs**: STI testing if PID suspected; cervical cytology/HPV testing if malignancy suspected.
- **Special Cases**: Laparoscopy for suspected endometriosis in fertility-desiring patients.

## Differential Diagnosis
### Gynecological
- Endometriosis, obstructive reproductive tract anomalies (imperforate hymen, OHVIRA syndrome), adnexal cysts/torsion, adenomyosis, PID, endometrial polyps, Asherman syndrome, ectopic pregnancy, membranous dysmenorrhea.

### Non-Gynecological
- Irritable bowel syndrome, urinary tract infections, interstitial cystitis, musculoskeletal causes (abdominal wall muscles, sacroiliac joints).

## Management / Treatment
### Nonpharmacological
- Heat application (as effective as NSAIDs), regular exercise, balanced diet rich in vitamins/minerals.
- Acupuncture/transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (limited evidence), behavioral counseling.
- Avoid spinal manipulation (no proven benefit).

### Pharmacological
- **First-line**: NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, fenamates like mefenamic acid) scheduled before menses.
- **Alternatives**: Acetaminophen (with pamabrom/caffeine), combined hormonal contraceptives (pills, patches, rings), progestin-only methods (for endometriosis-related secondary dysmenorrhea).
- **Second-line**: GnRH agonists/antagonists (e.g., leuprolide, elagolix), vasodilators (sildenafil), calcium channel blockers (nifedipine), antispasmodics (hyoscine butylbromide), magnesium (adjunct).

### Surgical
- Laparoscopy for endometriosis resection; endometrial ablation or hysterectomy for refractory cases. Uterine nerve transection not routinely recommended.

## Complications
- **Primary**: No direct complications but impacts daily activities and quality of life.
- **Secondary**: Infertility, pelvic organ prolapse, heavy bleeding, anemia (depending on etiology).

## Prognosis
- **Primary**: Generally good with NSAIDs; severe cases may require combination therapy.
- **Secondary**: Depends on underlying cause and treatment response. Reassessment recommended after 3–6 months of treatment.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Encourage balanced nutrition, regular exercise, and heat therapy.
- Educate on tracking symptoms and following up for uncontrolled symptoms.
- Highlight dietary factors (e.g., dairy intake, vitamins) linked to reduced pain severity.

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Multidisciplinary approach involving physicians, nurses, pharmacists, pelvic physical therapists, and other specialists.
- Prioritize patient-centered, evidence-based care with clear communication among team members to optimize outcomes and address unique patient needs.
