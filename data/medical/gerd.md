# Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

## Definition / Overview
- GERD is a condition characterized by retrograde flow of gastric contents into the esophagus or beyond (e.g., oral cavity, larynx, lungs), resulting in esophagitis and inflammation of affected mucosal surfaces.
- Defined by the American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) as chronic symptoms or mucosal damage due to abnormal reflux of gastric contents.
- Classifications include nonerosive reflux disease (NERD) and erosive reflux disease.

## Epidemiology
- Western countries: Prevalence 10–20%, with severe disease in 6%.
- Asian countries: Prevalence ~5%.
- Equal prevalence between men and women; Barrett esophagus more common in men (3:1 ratio).
- Increasing prevalence with age (peak incidence in individuals aged 60–70).
- Pediatric population: Incidence 12–50% in children aged 0–18 (2000–2005); universal in preterm infants.
- Pregnancy: Affects 20–30% in first trimester, 40–45% in second, and 60% in third trimester.

## Etiology / Causes
- **Anatomical/Physiological Defects**:
  - Transient lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation or low resting LES pressure.
  - Hiatus hernia.
  - Increased intra-abdominal pressure (e.g., obesity, pregnancy, ascites).
  - Impaired esophageal motility.
  - Impaired saliva production or esophageal mucosal defense.
- **Risk Factors**:
  - Male sex, white ethnicity.
  - Age ≥50, BMI >30, smoking, alcohol.
  - Dietary factors (fatty, spicy, acidic foods).
  - Medications (nitrates, calcium channel blockers, anticholinergics).
  - Delayed gastric emptying, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH).
  - Anxiety/depression (bidirectional relationship).

## Pathophysiology
- **Key Mechanisms**:
  - Transient LES relaxation (TRLES): Primary mechanism for reflux in healthy individuals and GERD.
  - Hiatal hernia: Disrupts LES-diaphragm relationship and acts as a gastric contents reservoir.
  - Increased intra-abdominal fat: Raises gastroesophageal pressure gradient and TRLES frequency.
  - Impaired esophageal defense: Reduced acid clearance (motility/saliva) and mucosal barrier dysfunction.
- **Inflammatory Pathways**:
  - Cytokine-mediated inflammation via hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-2α and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer (NF-κB).
  - Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6) and T-cell infiltration contribute to mucosal damage.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Typical Symptoms**:
  - Heartburn (burning sensation behind sternum, post-prandial, exacerbated by exercise/recumbency).
  - Regurgitation (sour/burning fluid in throat/mouth).
  - Noncardiac chest pain.
- **Atypical Symptoms**:
  - Chronic cough, asthma, dysphonia, laryngitis, sinusitis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.
  - Dysphagia (30% with peptic strictures/Schatzki rings).
- **Alarm Symptoms**:
  - Dysphagia, odynophagia, weight loss, anemia, hematemesis/melena.

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Initial Approach**:
  - Empiric PPI trial (8–12 weeks) for typical symptoms without alarm features.
  - Alarm symptoms necessitate endoscopy (EGD) and/or pH monitoring.
- **Diagnostic Tools**:
  - **PPI Trial**: Symptom response supports GERD diagnosis.
  - **Endoscopy (EGD)**: Los Angeles classification for esophagitis severity (A–D).
  - **Ambulatory pH Monitoring**:
    - Wireless (48–96-hour) or transnasal (24-hour) pH testing.
    - pH-impedance for nonacid reflux assessment.
  - **Manometry**: Preoperative assessment for motility disorders (e.g., achalasia).
- **Imaging**: Barium studies limited to detecting strictures/hernias; no role in uncomplicated GERD.

## Differential Diagnosis
- Coronary artery disease, infectious esophagitis, eosinophilic esophagitis, radiation/chemotherapy-induced esophagitis.
- Esophageal motility disorders (e.g., achalasia, diffuse spasm), benign strictures.
- Peptic ulcer disease, nonulcer dyspepsia, gastroparesis, gastric cancer.
- Rumination, biliary colic.

## Management / Treatment
- **Lifestyle Modifications**:
  - Weight loss (even modest amounts improve symptoms).
  - Elevate bed head, avoid meals 2–3 hours before bedtime.
  - Avoid trigger foods (chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods), smoking.
- **Pharmacological Therapy**:
  - **PPIs**: First-line (omeprazole, lansoprazole, etc.); standard/high doses (see Table for comparison).
  - **H2RAs**: Famotidine/cimetidine (ranitidine/nizatidine withdrawn due to NDMA).
  - **P-CABs**: Vonoprazan (potassium-competitive acid blocker; second-line for refractory cases).
  - Adjuncts: Baclofen (reduces TRLES), sucralfate (pregnancy).
- **Endoscopic/Surgical Options**:
  - **Stretta**: Radiofrequency ablation for refractory GERD (LA A/B, no large hernia).
  - **Magnetic Sphincter Augmentation**: titanium ring placement around LES.
  - **Transoral Incisionless Fundoplication (TIF)**: For mild/moderate refractory cases.
  - **Surgical**: Laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication, gastric bypass (obese patients).

## Complications
- **Esophageal**:
  - Severe esophagitis (LA C/D), peptic strictures, dysphagia.
  - Barrett esophagus (5–15% of reflux patients; progresses to adenocarcinoma in 5%).
- **Extraesophageal**:
  - Laryngitis, chronic cough, asthma, sinusitis, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.
  - Dental erosions.
- **Systemic**: Iron deficiency anemia, upper GI bleeding.

## Prognosis
- Most patients respond to medications but often require long-term maintenance.
- Relapse common after discontinuation of therapy.
- Surgical outcomes excellent in uncomplicated cases; morbidity higher in complex medical histories.
- Barrett esophagus increases adenocarcinoma risk (8-fold higher in men).

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Lifestyle modifications (weight management, dietary adjustments, sleep hygiene).
- Avoid known triggers (alcohol, caffeine, spicy foods).
- Regular follow-up for high-risk patients (Barrett esophagus, severe esophagitis).

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Collaboration between primary care clinicians and gastroenterologists improves outcomes.
- Multidisciplinary approach for refractory cases (e.g., surgery, nutrition, psychology).
