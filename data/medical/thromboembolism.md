# Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)

## Definition / Overview
- Venous thromboembolic (VTE) disease encompasses deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), representing the third leading cardiovascular diagnosis globally. It is associated with significant morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs.

## Epidemiology
- Annual incidence: 300,000–600,000 cases in the U.S., with 100,000–300,000 VTE-related deaths yearly. In Europe, >500,000 annual deaths.
- 60% of VTE cases are hospital-acquired, making it the leading preventable cause of death in hospitalized patients.

## Etiology / Causes
- **Patient factors**: Age >40 years, obesity, varicose veins, immobility, oral contraceptives, smoking.
- **Disease states**: Malignancy, spinal cord injury, nephrotic syndrome, congestive heart failure, inflammatory bowel disease, recent myocardial infarction.
- **Procedures**: Pelvic/hip fractures, orthopedic surgeries (hip/knee).
- **Hematologic disorders**: Factor V Leiden mutation, protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin III deficiency, lupus anticoagulant, dysfibrinogenemia, prothrombin mutation, polycythemia vera, paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria.
- **Other**: Pregnancy, validated risk models (Caprini, Padua, Briggs/Brigham and Women’s, IMPROVE, Premier).

## Pathophysiology
- Thrombus formation in deep veins (legs, pelvis, arms) may propagate proximally. Embolization to pulmonary vasculature causes gas exchange impairment, alveolar edema, or necrosis.
- Chronic recurrent PE may lead to pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale.
- Paradoxical embolism possible with right-to-left shunts (e.g., patent foramen ovale).

## Clinical Presentation
- **DVT**: Unilateral limb pain, swelling, warmth, tenderness (physical signs have low diagnostic yield).
- **PE**: Sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, cough, hemoptysis. Massive PE: syncope, hypotension, shock.
- **Physical exam**: Tachypnea (>18/min), tachycardia (>100/min), fever, rales, S3/S4 gallop, pleural friction rub.
- **Chronic VTE**: Pulmonary hypertension signs (right ventricular hypertrophy, tricuspid regurgitation, hepatomegaly, edema).

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Pretest probability**: Modified Wells criteria (score ≥4 indicates high probability).
- **Low probability**: D-dimer assay (high negative predictive value; age-adjusted cutoff for older adults).
- **High/moderate probability**: CTPA or VQ scan for PE; proximal/whole-leg ultrasound for DVT.
- **PERC rule**: Avoids D-dimer in low-risk PE if all criteria met (age <50, pulse <100, SpO₂ >94%, no leg swelling, hemoptysis, trauma/surgery, prior VTE, estrogen use).
- **Additional tests**: Echocardiography for right-heart strain in massive PE; ABG, troponin, BNP, ECG, chest X-ray have limited diagnostic utility.

## Differential Diagnosis
- **DVT**: Cellulitis, arterial insufficiency, lymphedema, hematoma.
- **PE**: Congestive heart failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pneumonia, myocardial infarction.

## Management / Treatment
- **Anticoagulation**: First-line (DOACs: apixaban, rivaroxaban preferred; warfarin requires bridging with heparin).
  - **Duration**: 3–6 months for initial VTE; 4–6 weeks for isolated distal DVT (low recurrence risk).
  - **Special populations**: 
    - Cancer: LMWH.
    - Pregnancy: LMWH (minimum 6 weeks postpartum, total 3 months).
    - Obesity: Use actual weight for LMWH dosing.
- **Thrombolytics**: For massive PE (hemodynamic instability) or DVT with compartment syndrome.
- **Vena cava filters**: Contraindications to anticoagulation only.
- **Outpatient management**: PESI score ≤65 or simplified criteria (age <80, no comorbidities, stable vitals).

## Complications
- **Long-term**: Post-thrombotic syndrome, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale.
- **Bleeding**: Most common complication (especially with advanced age, hepatic/renal dysfunction).
- **Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)**: Thrombosis with thrombocytopenia 5–10 days post-heparin exposure.
- **Warfarin-induced skin necrosis**: Rare (<0.1%), linked to protein C deficiency.

## Prognosis
- **Mortality**: 4.5% in-hospital for acute VTE; 10% with active cancer; 14% for unstable PE at 30 days.
- **Recurrence**: 17.7% at 5 years with cancer; 8.6% without.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- **Thromboprophylaxis**: Reduces VTE incidence by 30–65%. Betrixaban approved for extended prophylaxis in acutely ill medical patients.
- **Orthopedic surgery**: AAOS/ACCP guidelines recommend 10–14 days (or up to 35 days if low bleeding risk) of chemoprophylaxis (LMWH, fondaparinux, DOACs, warfarin, aspirin) ± mechanical methods.
- **Hospital protocols**: Interprofessional collaboration for VTE prevention and treatment protocols.

## Interprofessional Considerations
- **Team approach**: Clinicians, nurses, pharmacists, administrators required for protocol development, patient education, and adherence monitoring. Pharmacists ensure dosing accuracy and monitor compliance; nurses provide education and complication surveillance.
