# Gout

## Definition / Overview
- Gout is one of the most common causes of chronic inflammatory arthritis in the United States, characterized by deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) monohydrate crystals in joints and periarticular tissues.  
- Historically termed the "disease of kings and king of diseases," it is defined by MSU crystal formation due to hyperuricemia (serum uric acid >6.8 mg/dL/400 µmol/L).  
- First described by Hippocrates, it is a well-studied and manageable rheumatic disease.

## Epidemiology
- Prevalence: 1–4% globally; 3–6% in men vs. 1–2% in women in Western nations.  
- Rising incidence over recent decades, peaking in older adults but plateauing after age 70.  
- Higher prevalence in African American individuals (OR 1.81 in women, OR 1.26 in men) compared to White Americans.  
- Associated with comorbidities like obesity, hypertension, CKD, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

## Etiology / Causes
- Multifactorial: genetic predisposition (heritability of hyperuricemia ~73%), medical comorbidities (e.g., OSA, CKD), and dietary factors (high-purine foods, alcohol, fructose-rich beverages).  
- Genetic defects linked to hyperuricemia (e.g., HGPRT deficiency, PRPP synthetase overactivity).  
- Evolutionary loss of uricase gene in humans, leading to hyperuricemia.  
- Risk factors: diuretics, obesity, Mediterranean diet, sleep apnea, metabolic syndrome.

## Pathophysiology
- **Purine Metabolism**: Humans lack uricase, leading to uric acid accumulation. Xanthine oxidase converts xanthine to uric acid.  
- **Hyperuricemia**: Results from overproduction (e.g., tumor lysis, psoriasis) or underexcretion (renal dysfunction).  
- **Crystal Formation**: MSU crystals form in joints (e.g., first MTP joint) due to supersaturation, low temperature, pH changes, and local cation concentrations.  
- **Inflammatory Response**: MSU crystals activate NLRP3 inflammasome → IL-1β release, neutrophil recruitment, and cytokine cascade (TNF-α, IL-6).  
- **Chronic Stage**: Tophi develop as granulomatous deposits, causing joint erosion via osteoclast activation and cartilage degradation.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Acute Gout Flare**: Sudden, severe monoarticular pain (85–90% in lower extremities; 50% first MTP joint), erythema, swelling, and warmth. May mimic cellulitis or septic arthritis.  
- **Chronic Tophaceous Gout**: Subcutaneous tophi (chalk-like nodules) in ears, fingers, knees, and Achilles tendon.  
- **Systemic Symptoms**: Fever, leukocytosis, and malaise during flares.  
- **Atypical Presentations**: Polyarticular involvement (3–14% initial attacks), axial joint involvement (rare), and finger pad tophi in postmenopausal women.

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Synovial Fluid Analysis**: Gold standard. MSU crystals (needle-shaped, negatively birefringent under polarized light) detected in 100% of tophi and during intercritical periods.  
- **Laboratory Findings**: Elevated WBC, ESR, CRP during flares; serum urate levels may be normal or low during acute attacks.  
- **Imaging**: Ultrasound (double contour sign on cartilage; sensitivity 83%, specificity 76%), dual-energy CT (sensitivity 87%, specificity 84%) for MSU deposits.  
- **Diagnostic Criteria**: ACR/EULAR 2015 criteria (sensitivity 92%, specificity 89%).

## Differential Diagnosis
- **Acute Flare**: Calcium pyrophosphate crystal deposition (pseudogout), septic arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, osteoarthritis, cellulitis, trauma.  
- **Tophaceous Gout**: Dactylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteomyelitis.

## Management / Treatment
### Acute Flare
- **NSAIDs**: Indomethacin (50 mg TID), naproxen (500 mg BID), ibuprofen (800 mg TID). Avoid in CKD (CrCl <60 mL/min) or PUD.  
- **Colchicine**: Low-dose (1.8 mg day 1, then 0.6 mg daily); avoid IV due to toxicity.  
- **Corticosteroids**: Prednisolone 30–40 mg/day for 5–10 days; intra-articular for monoarticular flares.  
- **IL-1 Inhibitors**: Anakinra (100 mg SC daily × 3 days) or canakinumab (150 mg SC) for refractory cases.

### Prophylaxis
- **Colchicine**: 0.6 mg daily for 3–6 months when initiating urate-lowering therapy (ULT).  
- **Avoid**: Azathioprine, cyclosporine with colchicine due to toxicity risk.

### Urate-Lowering Therapy (ULT)
- **Xanthine Oxidase Inhibitors (XOIs)**:  
  - **Allopurinol**: Start 100 mg/day (50 mg/day in CKD); titrate to target serum urate <6 mg/dL. Avoid in HLA-B*5801 carriers (test in high-risk populations).  
  - **Febuxostat**: 40–80 mg/day; alternative for allopurinol intolerance. Monitor CV risk (CARES trial suggests increased mortality).  
- **Uricosurics**: Probenecid (250 mg BID–1000 mg TID); contraindicated in CKD (CrCl <60 mL/min) and nephrolithiasis.  
- **Pegloticase**: IV every 2 weeks for refractory gout; contraindicated in G6PD deficiency.  

### Lifestyle Modifications
- **Diet**: DASH diet, low purine intake (avoid seafood, red meat), limit alcohol/fructose.  
- **Weight Loss**: 5–10% reduction lowers serum urate.  
- **Vitamin C**: ≥500 mg/day reduces urate levels.  

## Complications
- **Skeletal**: Tophi, joint deformity, bone loss.  
- **Urological**: Urate nephropathy, nephrolithiasis.  
- **Ocular**: Conjunctivitis, uveitis, scleritis.  
- **Cardiovascular**: Increased risk of MI, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.86, 1.552, respectively).  

## Prognosis
- Varies with comorbidities. Early-onset flares correlate with severe disease.  
- Untreated gout leads to recurrent flares, tophi, and joint destruction.  
- OSA linked to hyperuricemia but not an independent cause of gout.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Educate on weight loss, dietary modifications (avoid high-purine foods/liquids), and adherence to ULT/prophylaxis.  
- Regular serum urate monitoring and flare management plans.  

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Collaborative care involving rheumatologists, primary care providers, pharmacists, and dietitians.  
- Pharmacists ensure medication safety (e.g., HLA testing for allopurinol, drug interactions).  
- Dietitians guide dietary changes; nurses assist in patient education and monitoring.  
- Referral to specialists for refractory cases, unclear diagnosis, or renal impairment.
