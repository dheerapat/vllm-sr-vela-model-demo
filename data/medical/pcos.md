# Polycystic Ovarian Syndrome  

## Definition / Overview  
- Most common endocrine disorder in females of reproductive age worldwide.  
- Involves insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, leading to disrupted hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis function.  
- Associated comorbidities: infertility, metabolic syndrome, obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular risks, depression, obstructive sleep apnea, endometrial cancer, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD).  
- Diagnosis requires exclusion of mimicking disorders.  

## Epidemiology  
- Prevalence: 5%–26% depending on diagnostic criteria.  
- Rotterdam criteria (2/3: hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, polycystic ovaries) are most commonly used.  
- Higher prevalence in Mexican Americans vs. non-Hispanic whites and African Americans.  
- Phenotypic changes over time: classic PCOS (phenotype A) decreases, with reduced hyperandrogenism and increased FSH levels.  

## Etiology / Causes  
- Multigenic disorder with epigenetic factors.  
- Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) linked genes to insulin resistance, ovarian steroidogenesis, and PI3K-Akt signaling.  
- Twin studies: ~70% heritability.  
- *DENND1A* gene identified as a risk marker.  
- Epigenetic factors: obesity-related hypermethylation of granulosa cells.  
- Potential roles: fetal androgen exposure, microRNA regulation of GLUT4.  

## Pathophysiology  
- Insulin resistance (70% of cases) → hyperinsulinemia → ovarian androgen excess.  
- Dysregulation of hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis: elevated LH, reduced FSH, altered AMH.  
- Theca cell overexpression of steroidogenic enzymes (e.g., P450c17) → androgen excess.  
- Chronic low-grade inflammation (elevated CRP).  
- Two-thirds of cases: functional ovarian hyperandrogenism (FOH) with 17-OHP overresponse.  

## Clinical Presentation  
- **Irregular menses**: Defined by postmenarche interval abnormalities (e.g., <21 or >35 days after 3 years postmenarche).  
- **Hyperandrogenism**: Hirsutism (modified Ferriman-Gallwey score ≥4–6), alopecia, acne.  
- **Phenotypes**: 7 categories (A–G) based on hyperandrogenism, hirsutism, oligo-anovulation, and polycystic ovaries.  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- **Rotterdam Criteria**: 2/3 of hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, or polycystic ovaries/AMH elevation.  
- **Exclusion**: Thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, congenital adrenal hyperplasia (test 17-OHP, ACTH stimulation).  
- **Hyperandrogenism workup**: Total/free testosterone (prefer liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry), androstenedione, DHEAS.  
- **Imaging**: Transvaginal ultrasound (follicles per ovary ≥20), AMH (not standalone for diagnosis).  
- **Adolescents**: Require hyperandrogenism + ovulatory dysfunction; avoid ultrasound/AMH.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Androgenic steroid use, hypothyroidism, late-onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia, idiopathic hirsutism, ovarian malignancies.  

## Management / Treatment  
- **Lifestyle Modification**: 5% weight loss improves metabolic health, ovulation, and symptoms.  
- **Pharmacologic**:  
  - **Hormonal contraceptives**: First-line for menstrual irregularities, hirsutism, acne (e.g., ethinyl estradiol with antiandrogenic progestin).  
  - **Metformin**: Improves menstrual cycles, insulin resistance; adjunct for infertility.  
  - **Antiandrogens**: Spironolactone for hirsutism unresponsive to contraceptives.  
  - **Infertility**: Letrozole first-line; clomiphene citrate + metformin if inadequate response.  
  - **GLP-1 agonists**: Off-label for weight loss, insulin sensitivity, ovulation improvement.  
- **Symptom-based**: Laser hair removal, topical eflornithine, myoinositol (fertility support).  

## Complications  
- **Infertility**: Linked to anovulation; 70% face conception challenges.  
- **Obstetric**: Higher risk of miscarriage, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia.  
- **Metabolic**: MASLD (3× increased risk), insulin resistance (33%–66%), type 2 diabetes.  
- **Psychiatric**: Increased depression, anxiety, suicide attempts.  
- **Cancers**: Endometrial hyperplasia/cancer from unopposed estrogen.  

## Prognosis  
- Hirsutism and acne decrease with age; ovarian volume/follicle number decline.  
- Ovulation improves with age, but fertility success rates drop (e.g., letrozole pregnancy rate 41%–64.8%).  
- Increased long-term risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, but no elevated all-cause mortality.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Early diagnosis and lifestyle changes to reduce comorbidities.  
- Weight management, regular screening for metabolic/cardiovascular issues, and mental health support.  
- Family history awareness and genetic counseling considerations.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- Multidisciplinary approach: Physicians, dermatologists, reproductive endocrinologists, dietitians, psychiatrists, pharmacists, and nurses.  
- Coordinated care for metabolic health, fertility, mental health, and cosmetic concerns.  
- Patient-centered education and support groups to address stigma and improve adherence.
