# Animal Bites  

## Definition / Overview  
- Animal bites pose significant clinical challenges due to risks of infection, tissue damage, and transmission of diseases such as rabies and tetanus. Injuries range from superficial abrasions to deep punctures or crushed tissue. Management requires evidence-based strategies for wound care, infection prevention, and prophylaxis.  

## Epidemiology  
- Approximately **4.5 million animal bites** occur annually in the US, with **750,000 requiring medical attention**.  
- **Dogs** account for **60–90%** of bites, **cats** for **5–20%**, and other species (e.g., rats, monkeys) are rare.  
- **Children** are commonly bitten on the **head/neck** (due to smaller stature), while **adults** often sustain hand/arm injuries.  
- **Infection rates**:  
  - Dog bites: **2–25%** (higher with comorbidities, hand/foot involvement, or delayed care).  
  - Cat bites: **~30%** (puncture wounds increase risk of osteomyelitis, septic arthritis).  
- **Rat-bite fever** carries **10–13% mortality** if untreated.  

## Etiology / Causes  
- **Domestic cats and dogs** are the primary sources of biting animals.  
- **Pathogens in dog bites**:  
  - Aerobic: *Pasteurella canis*, *Staphylococcus*, *Streptococcus*.  
  - Anaerobic: *Bacteroides*, *Capnocytophaga canimorsus* (higher risk in immunocompromised individuals).  
- **Pathogens in cat bites**:  
  - Aerobic: *Pasteurella multocida*, *Streptococcus*, *Staphylococcus*, *Bartonella henselae* (cat scratch disease).  
  - Anaerobic: *Fusobacterium*, *Capnocytophaga*.  
- **Less common animals**:  
  - **Rats**: *Streptobacillus moniliformis*, *Spirillum minus* (rat-bite fever), leptospirosis.  
  - **Monkeys**: Human-like oral flora (e.g., *Neisseria*, *Streptococcus*), herpes B virus.  
  - **Bats**: Rabies virus.  
- **Other infections**: Tularemia (*Francisella tularensis*), plague (*Yersinia pestis*), fungal infections.  

## Pathophysiology  
- **Mechanical trauma**: Teeth cause punctures, lacerations, crush injuries, or fractures.  
- **Infection mechanisms**: Polymicrobial contamination from oral flora (aerobes/anaerobes) and environmental/skin bacteria.  
- **Systemic spread**: Pathogens may travel via blood (sepsis, endocarditis) or nerves (rabies virus binding to neuromuscular junctions, ascending to the brain).  

## Clinical Presentation  
- **General signs of infection**: Erythema, swelling, warmth, purulent drainage, lymphangitis.  
- **Specific presentations**:  
  - **Rabies**: Myalgia, fever, paresthesia, confusion, hallucinations, coma.  
  - **Cat scratch disease**: Papule at bite site, lymphadenopathy, myalgia, fever.  
  - **Rat-bite fever**: Fever, headache, maculopapular rash, arthritis, possible multi-organ involvement.  
  - **Tularemia**: Fever, headache, weakness.  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- **Laboratory tests**:  
  - Wound cultures (if infected).  
  - Blood cultures (systemic infection).  
  - CBC (leukocytosis suggests infection).  
- **Imaging**:  
  - X-rays: Fractures, foreign bodies, gas (anaerobic infection).  
  - Advanced imaging (CT/MRI): Suspected deep tissue abscess or complications.  
- **Risk assessment**: Tetanus immunization status, rabies exposure risk.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Skin infections (cellulitis, abscesses, necrotizing fasciitis).  
- Insect bites (e.g., brown recluse spider, ticks).  
- Human bites or self-inflicted injuries.  
- Child abuse or domestic violence (history mismatch).  
- Chronic ulcers (e.g., *Mycobacterium marinum*, autoimmune conditions like pyoderma gangrenosum).  
- Crush injuries, burns, or vasculitis.  

## Management / Treatment  
- **Wound care**:  
  - Thorough irrigation (high-pressure syringe).  
  - Debridement of devitalized tissue.  
  - Delayed closure for high-risk wounds; primary closure only for cosmetic concerns (e.g., face).  
- **Antibiotics**:  
  - **Prophylaxis (penetration ≥epidermis)**: Amoxicillin-clavulanate (first-line).  
    - Penicillin-allergic: Doxycycline + metronidazole or clindamycin + TMP-SMX.  
  - **Systemic infection**: Hospitalization, IV antibiotics (ampicillin-sulbactam).  
  - **Specific pathogens**:  
    - *Bartonella henselae*: Azithromycin.  
    - *Francisella tularensis*: Streptomycin/gentamicin (severe), doxycycline (mild).  
    - Rat-bite fever: Penicillin G/ceftriaxone.  
- **Prophylaxis**:  
  - Tetanus: Update if last dose >5 years; immunoglobulin if >10 years.  
  - Rabies: Vaccine + immunoglobulin (WHO guidelines: days 0, 3, 7, 14) for high-risk exposures.  

## Complications  
- Infectious: Cellulitis, abscess, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis, sepsis, meningitis, endocarditis.  
- Non-infectious: Nerve damage, tendon rupture, psychological effects (PTSD).  
- MRSA transmission between animals and humans.  

## Prognosis  
- Generally excellent with prompt intervention.  
- Dog bites cause **~30–50 annual fatalities** in the US.  
- Increasing severity of injuries requiring hospitalization/surgery.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Responsible pet ownership and avoiding provocation of unfamiliar animals.  
- Tetanus vaccination updates.  
- Rabies preexposure prophylaxis for high-risk individuals (e.g., veterinarians).  
- Immediate wound irrigation with soap/water after a bite.  
- Recognize signs of infection (redness, swelling, pus) and seek care.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- Collaborative care involving clinicians, nurses, pharmacists, infectious disease specialists, surgeons, and public health officials.  
- Clinicians: Diagnose, manage wounds, assess rabies/tetanus risk.  
- Nurses: Wound care, patient education, monitoring for complications.  
- Pharmacists: Antibiotic selection, dosing, drug interaction advice.  
- Coordination ensures holistic care, especially for complex cases.
