# Vaginal Candidiasis

## Definition / Overview
- Vulvovaginitis caused by *Candida* species, most commonly *Candida albicans*, characterized by inflammation of the vulva and vagina.

## Epidemiology
- Accounts for approximately one-third of vulvovaginitis cases in reproductive-aged women.  
- Lifetime prevalence: ~70% of women report at least one episode.  
- Recurrent cases (≥4 episodes/year): ~8% of women.  
- *C. albicans* causes 90% of cases; *C. glabrata* accounts for most remaining cases.  
- Underreported due to over-the-counter treatment availability; epidemiological data limited by inconsistent diagnosis methods.

## Etiology / Causes
- *Candida* species (primarily *C. albicans* and *C. glabrata*).  
- Risk factors:  
  - Estrogen use (e.g., oral contraceptives, hormone therapy).  
  - Elevated endogenous estrogens (e.g., pregnancy, obesity).  
  - Diabetes mellitus.  
  - Immunosuppression (e.g., chemotherapy, HIV, transplant).  
  - Broad-spectrum antibiotic use.  
- Not sexually transmitted, though more common in sexually active women.  
- Recurrent cases may involve genetic susceptibility to fungal infections.

## Pathophysiology
- *Candida* species penetrate the vaginal mucosa, triggering an inflammatory response.  
- Inflammatory cells: Polymorphonuclear cells and macrophages.  
- Results in symptoms such as irritation, discharge, and edema due to mucosal inflammation.

## Clinical Presentation
- Vulvar/vaginal erythema, excoriations, thick white adherent discharge, and swelling.  
- Symptoms: Itching, burning, dysuria, dyspareunia.  
- Often worsens premenstrually.  
- *C. glabrata* infections typically present with milder symptoms.  
- Cervix is usually normal (no cervical motion tenderness or abnormal discharge).

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Clinical findings**: Vulvar/vaginal erythema, discharge, and inflammation.  
- **Diagnostic tests**:  
  - Vaginal wet prep: Budding yeast, hyphae, or pseudohyphae.  
  - pH testing: Typically <5.  
  - Potassium hydroxide (KOH) microscopy: Visualize fungal elements.  
  - Fungal culture or PCR (for recurrent/non-responsive cases).  
- Rule out bacterial vaginosis (clue cells, positive Whiff test) and trichomoniasis.  
- Cultures unnecessary for straightforward *C. albicans* cases but indicated for recurrent symptoms.

## Differential Diagnosis
- Allergic reaction  
- Atopic dermatitis  
- Lichen sclerosis  
- Lichen simplex chronicus  
- Neoplasm  
- Paget disease  
- Physiologic leukorrhea  
- Psoriasis  
- Sexual abuse  
- Vulvodynia  

## Management / Treatment
- **Acute uncomplicated candidiasis**:  
  - Single-dose fluconazole 150 mg oral or terconazole intravaginally (1–7 days).  
- **Complicated/recurrent candidiasis**:  
  - Extended azole therapy (e.g., intravaginal azole for ≥1 week or fluconazole 150 mg every 3 days × 3 doses).  
  - Suppressive therapy: Weekly fluconazole for 6 months (non-pregnant).  
- **Pregnant patients**: 7-day intravaginal azole (avoid oral fluconazole).  
- **Breastfeeding**: Fluconazole considered safe.  
- **Non-*albicans* species** (e.g., *C. glabrata*): Boric acid 600 mg vaginally for ≥14 days.  
- **Avoid**: Non-prescribed douches, yogurt, garlic, or probiotics (ineffective per evidence).

## Complications
- Rare but may include severe vulvar erythema, edema, excoriations, fissures.  
- Antifungal adverse effects: Nausea, abdominal pain, headache, rash, alopecia.

## Prognosis
- Recurrence rate: 14–28% in immunocompetent individuals.  
- Recurrence often due to persistent/exogenous *Candida* strains or genetic susceptibility.  
- Triggers: Antibiotics, sexual activity, dietary factors.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Keep vaginal area dry (change wet clothing post-exercise/swimming).  
- Avoid irritants (e.g., scented soaps, douches).  
- Use breathable fabrics.  
- Consider prophylactic antifungals during antibiotic use.  
- Avoid over-the-counter treatments without confirmation to prevent delayed diagnosis.

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Managed by gynecologists, primary care clinicians, nurse practitioners.  
- Pharmacists assist with therapy selection and patient counseling.  
- Multidisciplinary approach for complicated cases (e.g., immunosuppressed patients).  
- Emphasize broad differential diagnosis and thorough evaluation for treatment failure.
