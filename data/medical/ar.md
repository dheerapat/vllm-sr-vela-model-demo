# Allergic Rhinitis

## Definition / Overview
- Atopic disease characterized by symptoms of sneezing, nasal congestion, clear rhinorrhea, postnasal drip, and nasal pruritis. It is an IgE-mediated immune response with an immediate phase (IgE, mast cell degranulation) and a late phase (leukotriene-mediated, cytokine release, eosinophil infiltration). Classified as seasonal (intermittent) or perennial (chronic). Associated with systemic allergic conditions (e.g., asthma, atopic dermatitis).

## Epidemiology
- Prevalence: 15% based on physician diagnosis, up to 30% based on symptoms. Peaks in the second to fourth decades, gradually declines with age. Common in children (14.6% in 13–14-year-olds, 8.5% in 6–7-year-olds). Seasonal AR more common in pediatric populations, chronic in adults.
- Economic impact: 3.6% of adults miss work; 36% report impaired work performance. Indirect costs (lost productivity) dominate the cost burden.
- Risk factors: Family history of atopy, male sex, allergen-specific IgE, serum IgE >100 IU/mL before age 6, higher socioeconomic status. Early exposure to foods/formula, cigarette smoking in infancy may increase risk. "Farm effect" (40% lower risk in those raised on farms) and potential protective effects of breastfeeding (debated) noted.

## Etiology / Causes
- IgE-mediated response to inhaled antigens (e.g., pollens, animal dander, dust mites). Genetic factors: 45–60% concordance in monozygotic twins, 25% in dizygotic twins. Chromosomal regions 3 and 4 linked to allergic responses.

## Pathophysiology
- **Early phase (5–15 minutes post-exposure):** IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation releases histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins. Histamine causes sneezing (via trigeminal nerve), rhinorrhea, and nasal congestion.
- **Late phase (4–6 hours post-exposure):** Cytokine release (IL-4, IL-13) leads to eosinophil, T-lymphocyte, and basophil infiltration, causing nasal edema and congestion.
- Non-IgE-mediated hyperresponsiveness due to eosinophilic infiltration and mucosal obliteration; triggers include smoke, cold air.

## Clinical Presentation
- Symptoms: Sneezing, nasal congestion, clear rhinorrhea, postnasal drip, nasal pruritis. Associated features: Allergic conjunctivitis, non-productive cough, Eustachian tube dysfunction, chronic sinusitis.
- Physical findings: Mouth breathing, frequent sniffling, transverse supra-tip nasal crease, allergic shiners (dark circles under eyes), nasal mucosa swelling, thin clear secretions, bluish inferior turbinates, cobblestoning. Endoscopic exam may reveal polyps or structural abnormalities.

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- Clinical diagnosis based on history and physical. Confirmatory tests: Serum allergen-specific IgE or skin prick testing (reserved for cases unresponsive to empiric treatment). Skin testing preferred for sensitivity and cost-effectiveness. Contraindications: Uncontrolled asthma, unstable cardiovascular disease, pregnancy, beta-blockers, certain medications (H2 antagonists, tricyclics, omalizumab).
- Imaging (e.g., CT) not routine; used to rule out sinusitis or structural issues.

## Differential Diagnosis
- Vasomotor rhinitis, infectious rhinitis, cerebrospinal fluid leak, non-allergic rhinitis with eosinophilia (NARES), chemical rhinitis, rhinitis of pregnancy, drug-induced rhinitis (e.g., NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors), autoimmune/granulomatous causes (e.g., granulomatosis with polyangiitis), nasal polyposis, neoplasms. Congenital causes (choanal atresia) in children <2 years.

## Management / Treatment
- **Avoidance:** Limit exposure to allergens (e.g., allergen-impermeable bedding, HEPA filters, pet isolation). Not always practical.
- **Pharmacological:**
  - **First-line:** Intranasal corticosteroids (beclomethasone, budesonide, fluticasone, mometasone, triamcinolone). Administered with proper technique (aim laterally, avoid septum). Peak effect in days.
  - **Antihistamines:** First-gen (diphenhydramine; sedating, anticholinergic side effects) and second-gen (fexofenadine, loratadine, desloratadine, cetirizine; less sedating).
  - **Intranasal antihistamines (azelastine):** Rapid onset, used with steroids.
  - **Leukotriene receptor antagonists (montelukast, zafirlukast):** Adjunct for severe/refractory cases.
  - **Decongestants:** Oral (pseudoephedrine) or intranasal (xylometazoline); avoid prolonged use (risk of rhinitis medicamentosa).
  - **Immunotherapy:** Subcutaneous (SCIT) or sublingual (SLIT) for 3–5 years. Omalizumab (anti-IgE) for select cases.
  - **Saline irrigation:** Isotonic (adults) or hypertonic (children).
- **Surgical:** For nasal polyps, turbinate hypertrophy, or refractory sinusitis. Budesonide FDA-approved for pregnancy.

## Complications
- Chronic rhinosinusitis, nasal polyps (benign, bilateral; unilateral raises malignancy concern), adenoid hypertrophy, Eustachian tube dysfunction, asthma (10–40% comorbidity), otitis media with effusion, eosinophilic esophagitis. Immunotherapy risks: Exacerbation of rhinitis/asthma, anaphylaxis (requires emergency preparedness).

## Prognosis
- Symptoms often improve with age. Longitudinal study: 54.9% improved at 23-year follow-up (41.6% symptom-free). Younger onset associated with better outcomes. Immunotherapy (e.g., grass pollen) shows sustained benefit 3 years post-treatment.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Proper nasal spray administration technique (avoid septum, no deep inhalation). Allergen avoidance counseling. Importance of adhering to treatment due to asthma association. Educate on when to seek care (e.g., refractory symptoms, suspected malignancy).

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Primary care physicians manage most cases. Referrals to allergists/ENT for immunotherapy, nasal polyps, suspected malignancy, or CSF leaks. Team includes nurses, pharmacists, and specialists for co-management. Global access to specialists varies. Emphasis on self-management education and timely referrals.
