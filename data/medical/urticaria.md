# Chronic Urticaria  

## Definition / Overview  
- Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) is defined as the occurrence of wheals and/or angioedema for ≥6 weeks. Synonymous with "chronic urticaria" and "chronic idiopathic urticaria." A mast cell-mediated condition associated with significant quality-of-life impairment.  

## Epidemiology  
- Prevalence: 0.23% to 1.8% (U.S. and international).  
- Female predominance (2:1 ratio).  
- Highest prevalence in adults aged 40–60 years; affects both children and adults.  

## Etiology / Causes  
- **Autoimmune dysfunction**: Autoantibodies targeting IgE or IgE receptors in 40% of cases (positive autologous serum skin test [ASST]).  
- **Associated autoimmune disorders**: Hypothyroidism (9.8%), rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus, Sjögren syndrome, celiac disease, type 1 diabetes.  
- **Infections**: *Helicobacter pylori*, streptococci, staphylococci, *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, hepatitis viruses, *Giardia lamblia* (causality unproven; potential autoimmune or molecular mimicry links).  
- **Foods/additives**: Rarely causative.  
- **Malignancy**: Conflicting evidence; no established association.  

## Pathophysiology  
- Mast cell and basophil activation → histamine release → vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, dermal edema, and inflammatory cell recruitment.  
- **Immunological activation**: Type 2 (IgG), type 3 (immune complexes), and type 4 (T-cell) hypersensitivity may contribute.  
- **Non-immunological activation**: Reduced threshold for activation by physical/environmental triggers.  
- **Histopathology**: Intervascular edema in upper/mid-dermis, mixed perivascular infiltrates, dilated venules. No leukocytoclasis or vasculitis.  

## Clinical Presentation  
- **Wheals**: Pruritic, pink-to-red papules/plaques (central pallor), fleeting (<24 hours), no residual ecchymosis/pigmentation. May coalesce; pressure-prone areas often involved.  
- **Angioedema**: Occurs in 40%, pain/discomfort > pruritus, resolves in 72 hours, affects lips, peri-orbital, genitals, extremities.  
- **Secondary changes**: Excoriations, erosions, hemorrhagic crust from scratching.  

## Evaluation / Diagnosis  
- **History**: Duration, triggers, systemic symptoms, personal/family history of allergies/autoimmune disease, physical factors, medications, foods.  
- **Physical exam**: Document lesion duration via skin marking pen.  
- **Laboratory tests**: CBC, CRP/ESR (rule out systemic disease). Eosinophilia suggests atopy/parasites.  
- **Testing**: Skin prick testing not useful. ASST and basophil histamine release assay (BHRA) for autoantibodies (research context).  
- **Tools**: Urticaria activity score, control test, quality-of-life index.  

## Differential Diagnosis  
- Urticarial vasculitis (wheals >24h, pain, ecchymosis).  
- Papular urticaria (insect bite reaction, persistent papules).  
- Mastocytosis (Darier sign positive).  
- Auto-inflammatory syndromes (e.g., CAPS, Schnitzler).  
- C1-esterase inhibitor deficiency (isolated angioedema).  
- Bullous pemphigoid (pre-bullous pruritus/urticaria).  
- Anaphylaxis (acute, life-threatening).  

## Management / Treatment  
- **First-line**: Second-generation H1-antihistamines (e.g., cetirizine, loratadine) daily; dose ↑ to 4× standard if needed.  
- **Second-line**: Omalizumab (300 mg SC q4 weeks) for H1-refractory cases.  
- **Third-line**: Ciclosporin (off-label; minimize dose/duration due to nephrotoxicity/hypertension risks).  
- **Acute flares**: Short-course systemic corticosteroids.  
- **Not recommended**: Topical corticosteroids.  
- **Alternatives**: Limited evidence for leukotriene antagonists, dapsone, methotrexate, etc.  

## Complications  
- Severe pruritus disrupts sleep and quality of life.  

## Prognosis  
- Self-limited; mean duration 3–5 years.  
- 80% remission within 12 months.  
- 14% persist >5 years; predictors: thyroid autoimmunity, concurrent angioedema.  

## Prevention / Patient Education  
- Avoid NSAIDs/aspirin, triggers (tight clothing, heat).  
- Use H1-antihistamines regularly (not PRN).  
- Educate on symptom management and flares.  

## Interprofessional Considerations  
- Refer to dermatologist/clinical immunologist for H1-refractory cases.  
- Monitor treatment efficacy every 3–6 months.  
- Address psychological burden with counseling/psychotherapy as needed.
