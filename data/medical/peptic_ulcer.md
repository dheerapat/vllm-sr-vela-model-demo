# Peptic Ulcer Disease

## Definition / Overview
- Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) involves discontinuation in the inner lining of the gastrointestinal (GI) tract due to gastric acid secretion or pepsin, extending into the muscularis propria layer. Common locations include the stomach, proximal duodenum, lower esophagus, distal duodenum, or jejunum.

## Epidemiology
- Lifetime risk: 5–10%. 
- Decreasing global incidence due to improved hygiene, effective treatments, and judicious NSAID use. 
- Duodenal ulcers are four times more common than gastric ulcers. 
- Duodenal ulcers are more prevalent in men than women.

## Etiology / Causes
### Common:
- *Helicobacter pylori* infection
- NSAID use
- Medications (corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, potassium chloride, fluorouracil)

### Rare:
- Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
- Malignancy (gastric/lung cancer, lymphomas)
- Stress (acute illness, burns, head injury)
- Viral infection
- Vascular insufficiency
- Radiation therapy
- Crohn’s disease
- Chemotherapy

### Hypersecretory conditions:
- Zollinger-Ellison syndrome
- Systemic mastocytosis
- Cystic fibrosis
- Hyperparathyroidism
- Antral G cell hyperplasia

## Pathophysiology
- Imbalance between gastric mucosal protective factors (bicarbonate, mucus) and destructive factors (acid, pepsin).
- Risk factors: *H. pylori* infection, NSAID use, first-degree family history of PUD, emigration from a developing nation, African American/Hispanic ethnicity.
- *H. pylori*: Colonizes gastric mucosa, impairs bicarbonate secretion, induces inflammation, and causes gastric metaplasia.
- Histopathology: Gastric ulcers typically on the lesser curvature (antrum/fundus); duodenal ulcers in the duodenal bulb. Ulcers >5 mm with smooth/irregular borders.

## Clinical Presentation
- **Epigastric pain**: 
  - Gastric ulcer: 15–30 minutes post-meal.
  - Duodenal ulcer: 2–3 hours post-meal; nocturnal pain.
- Associated symptoms: Bloating, abdominal fullness, nausea/vomiting, weight loss/gain.
- **Alarm symptoms** (require urgent referral):
  - Unintentional weight loss
  - Progressive dysphagia
  - Overt GI bleeding
  - Iron deficiency anemia
  - Recurrent emesis
  - Family history of upper GI malignancy

## Evaluation / Diagnosis
- **Endoscopy**: 
  - Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD): Gold standard (90% sensitivity/specificity).
  - Indicated for patients >50 years, new-onset dyspeptic symptoms, or alarm symptoms.
- **H. pylori testing**:
  - Urea breath test (high sensitivity/specificity; post-treatment confirmation at 4–6 weeks).
  - Stool antigen test.
  - Serologic testing (antibodies).
  - Endoscopic biopsy (culture if refractory or antibiotic resistance suspected; 4–6 sites).
- **Imaging**:
  - Barium swallow if EGD contraindicated.
  - CT abdomen with contrast for complications (perforation, obstruction).
- **Labs**: CBC, LFTs, amylase/lipase, serum gastrin (Zollinger-Ellison suspicion).

## Differential Diagnosis
- Gastritis
- GERD
- Gastric cancer
- Pancreatitis
- Biliary colic/cholecystitis
- Myocardial infarction (inferior wall/right ventricular involvement)
- Mesenteric ischemia/vasculitis

## Management / Treatment
### Medical Therapy:
- **Antisecretory agents**:
  - Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs; first-line over H2-receptor antagonists).
  - Long-term PPI use requires calcium supplementation (fracture risk).
- **H. pylori eradication**:
  - Triple therapy: PPI + two antibiotics (clarithromycin, metronidazole, or amoxicillin) for 7–14 days.
  - Quadruple therapy (bismuth-based) if first-line fails.
- **NSAID-associated PUD**:
  - Discontinue NSAIDs or switch to lower dose.
  - Prostaglandin analogs (misoprostol) for prophylaxis.
- **Other medications**: Avoid corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, anticoagulants if possible.

### Surgical Therapy:
- Indicated for refractory ulcers (>5 mm after 8–12 weeks of PPI), noncompliance, or complications.
- Procedures: Vagotomy, partial gastrectomy.

## Complications
- Upper gastrointestinal bleeding
- Gastric outlet obstruction
- Perforation
- Penetration
- Gastric cancer

## Prognosis
- Excellent after successful treatment of underlying cause.
- Recurrence rates: >60% in most series.
- NSAID-induced gastric perforation rate: 0.3% per patient per year.
- Mortality rates have significantly decreased.

## Prevention / Patient Education
- Avoid NSAIDs, alcohol, tobacco, caffeine.
- Use lowest effective NSAID dose with prophylaxis (e.g., misoprostol) if necessary.
- Weight loss (association with obesity).
- Stress reduction counseling.

## Interprofessional Considerations
- Primary care providers, emergency physicians, nurses, pharmacists, and dietitians collaborate for comprehensive care.
- Nurses: Monitor patients, provide education, update team on status.
- Pharmacists: Ensure medication compliance and explain regimens.
- Dietitians: Address obesity as a potential trigger.
- Coordination improves outcomes and reduces morbidity.
